# African Resuscitation & Emergency Medicine Reference: Standard Consensus vs. Adapted Practice

## Section 1: Core Emergency Medicine & Resuscitation (Guidelines 1-13)

### 1. World Health Organization (WHO) — Emergency Care Toolkit & Basic Emergency Care (BEC)
*   **The Global Standard**: Focuses on structured, rapid syndromic identification and management of life-threatening airway, breathing, circulation, and disability (ABCD) abnormalities in resource-limited facilities [1, BEC].
*   **The African Reality**: Severe staffing shortages, lack of pulse oximetry, and inconsistent oxygen supplies [10, 28]. 
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: Focus on the WHO BEC "syndromic algorithm" rather than etiologic diagnoses. Standard clinical signs (respiratory rate, chest in-drawing, capillary refill time) replace lab-guided triage metrics [10, 69].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **SATS (South African Triage Scale)** clinical validation studies showing SATS successfully reduces wait-time and mortality in busy public EDs [191].

### 2. International Federation for Emergency Medicine (IFEM) — International ED Standards & Quality
*   **The Global Standard**: Recommends rigorous ED structural quality metrics, specialized pediatric emergency areas, and formalized emergency residency pathways.
*   **The African Reality**: The majority of emergency departments across Africa are staffed by generalists or family medicine doctors without formal emergency training [191].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: Implement structured training programs like the **Colleges of Medicine of South Africa (CMSA) Higher Diploma in Emergency Medicine [H Dip Emerg Med(SA)]** to empower non-specialists with core critical-care and resuscitation skills [28, CMSA Regulations].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **Mthatha Regional Hospital Sepsis Cohort Study (2017)** which prospectively demonstrated a 30-day mortality of nearly 25% for undifferentiated acute infections in EDs staffed by generalists [99, 191, 195].

### 3. American College of Emergency Physicians (ACEP) — Clinical Policies
*   **The Global Standard**: Evidence-based clinical guidelines on acute presentations (e.g., chest pain, dyspnea, agitation) recommending early, high-resource diagnostic pathways.
*   **The African Reality**: Advanced diagnostics (CT scans, troponins, ventilation) are heavily rationed or completely unavailable [10, 190].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: Utilize standardized risk-stratification scores (e.g., modified Wells' for PE, HEART score for chest pain) but with local thresholds; restrict advanced testing like Computed Tomography Pulmonary Angiography (CTPA) to high-test-probability patients [10].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **PROST (Procalcitonin in Sepsis Trial)** or local utility audits of clinical surrogates versus high-cost biomarkers in resource-limited settings [190, 256].

### 4. Royal College of Emergency Medicine (RCEM) — Clinical Standards
*   **The Global Standard**: Establishes strict audit targets for resuscitation times, procedural sedation safety, and emergency care delivery.
*   **The African Reality**: High patient volume, delayed presentations, and severe bed blockages in emergency units [191].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: Focus audits on low-resource safety indicators (e.g., time to first dose of antibiotics in open fractures, administration of paracetamol in acute painful progressive swelling) [34, 42, 81].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **Eastern Cape Infectious Disease Cohort (2020)** highlighting that incomplete vital sign recording at triage directly correlates with missed critical illness and mortality [191, 198].

### 5. National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) — Condition-Specific & Service-Delivery Guidance
*   **The Global Standard**: Promotes exhaustive diagnostic and therapeutic protocols for major medical presentations (e.g., sepsis, acute heart failure, asthma).
*   **The African Reality**: NICE guidelines assume consistent, universal access to high-resource intensive care units (ICUs) and specialized medical departments.
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: South African public facilities utilize condition-specific guidelines from the National Department of Health (NDoH) and Essential Drugs List (EDL) which strip NICE recommendations down to basic, accessible therapeutic agents [2, 59].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **National Department of Health EDL Audits** demonstrating that adherence to simplified, local primary-care EML guidelines significantly improves treatment consistency [59, 78].

### 6. American College of Radiology Appropriateness Criteria (ACR-AC) — Selection of Imaging
*   **The Global Standard**: Recommends advanced imaging (CT, MRI, ultrasound) as first-line diagnostic tools for specific presenting complaints (e.g., CT head for minor head injury, MRI spine for cord compression).
*   **The African Reality**: CT and MRI scans are either unavailable at the district level or subject to massive backlogs and transport delays [273, 276].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: Rely on clinical examination (GCS, focal signs) [114] and standard plain radiography (Chest X-ray, pelvic views) [63, 273]. Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) is heavily utilized as a rapid, bedside diagnostic adjunct [101, 276].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **Grey's Hospital Trauma CT Audits** illustrating that implementing clinical "hard signs" for stable penetrating trauma before imaging drastically saves costs and resources [74, 75].

### 7. International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR) — Evidence Reviews & Treatment Recommendations
*   **The Global Standard**: Establishes global consensus on cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) algorithms, therapeutic hypothermia, and advanced airway management.
*   **The African Reality**: Post-resuscitation care (continuous EEG, target temperature management, early coronary intervention) is unachievable in most public hospitals.
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: Resuscitation focus shifts toward high-quality manual chest compressions, early defibrillation, and rapid reversible cause identification (such as correcting severe hypoglycemia or hyperkalemia in metabolic emergencies) [216, 278].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **AMBITION-cm** (on a systems level) showing how streamlining acute resuscitation protocols to fit available monitoring capabilities saves lives [116].

### 8. European Resuscitation Council (ERC) Guidelines 2025 — Resuscitation Standards
*   **The Global Standard**: Mandates advanced CPR and post-cardiac arrest care, emphasizing end-tidal CO2 (ETCO2) monitoring and immediate mechanical ventilation.
*   **The African Reality**: Intubation is highly restricted due to the lack of mechanical ventilators and trained critical-care staff [10, 190].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: Prioritize non-invasive ventilation (bag-valve-mask with peep valve, supraglottic devices like i-Gel) [36, 152] and maintain a low threshold for utilizing surgical airways when conventional intubation fails [152].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **Grey's Hospital ICU Outcome Audits** indicating that ICU admission delays from the ED due to ventilator shortages are associated with steep increases in patient mortality [244, 257].

### 9. American Heart Association (AHA) CPR and Emergency Cardiovascular Care Guidelines 2025
*   **The Global Standard**: Outlines ACLS/PALS protocols, including targeted temperature management and early advanced airway placement.
*   **The African Reality**: High prevalence of severe cardiotoxic exposures (e.g., traditional medicine bufadienolides, pesticide poisoning) that mimic standard cardiac arrest but require radically different pharmacological reversal [29, 38].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: Avoid succinylcholine (suxamethonium) during airway control in neurotoxic snakebites or organophosphate poisoning due to prolonged apnea risks [152, 153]. Prioritize atropine and rapid antidote titration over generic cardiac algorithms [153, 189].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **SASS (South African Snakebite Symposium) Resuscitation Guidelines** which warn that standard paralytics like suxamethonium are contraindicated in neurotoxic envenomation [152, 153].

### 10. Surviving Sepsis Campaign (SSC) — Adult and Paediatric Sepsis Resuscitation
*   **The Global Standard**: Mandates the execution of a rigid **1-hour bundle** [17, 68]: measure lactate, obtain blood cultures, administer broad-spectrum antibiotics, and deliver a rapid crystalloid fluid bolus of **at least 30 mL/kg** for hypotension or lactate $\ge 4$ mmol/L [17, 18, 68].
*   **The African Reality**: Only **1.2% to 1.5% of African facilities** possess the resources to implement the SSC guidelines in their entirety [28, 44, 45]. Unselected aggressive fluid boluses (30 mL/kg) in patients with high baseline rates of HIV and chronic tuberculosis lead to fluid overload, acute respiratory failure, and death due to a lack of ICU ventilators [1, 10, 68].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: Implement the **Turkana ASAP (Assess, Stabilise, Administer, Prioritise) Sepsis framework** [10, 17]. This replaces the 1-hour bundle with a pragmatic, clinical response: small targeted fluid boluses (4 mL/kg or 250–500 mL) [70], bedside Capillary Refill Time (CRT) instead of lactate [70, 241], and Passive Leg Raise (PLR) with >15% pulse pressure rise to dynamically assess fluid responsiveness [69, 70, 236].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **FEAST Trial (2011/2012)**: Multi-center African RCT demonstrating a **45% relative increase in mortality** in children receiving rapid fluid boluses compared to maintenance fluids [18, 199]. Also, the **ANDROMEDA-SHOCK Trial (2019)** which validated CRT-targeted resuscitation as clinically equivalent (and often superior in organ protection) to lactate-guided resuscitation [243].

### 11. Society of Critical Care Medicine (SCCM) — Airway, Sedation, Shock, Sepsis, and Analgesia
*   **The Global Standard**: Focuses on advanced, continuous infusions of short-acting sedatives, analgesics, and neuromuscular blockers under close invasive hemodynamic monitoring in the ICU.
*   **The African Reality**: resurgence of critical illness on ward beds due to extreme ICU bed deficits [244, 257].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: Resuscitation and sedation in the ED must rely on cheap, long-acting, hemodynamically stable agents like **ketamine (1–2 mg/kg/hour)** for post-intubation sedation and analgesia, avoiding drugs that depress cardiac output or require rapid infusion pumps [36].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **South African Surgical Sepsis Audit (Smith et al., 2019)** which showed that compliance with standard high-resource critical-care bundles did not translate into improved outcomes for surgical sepsis patients in public hospitals [45, 88, 91].

### 12. European Society of Intensive Care Medicine (ESICM) — Critical-Care Guidelines
*   **The Global Standard**: Recommends early initiation of renal replacement therapy (RRT), extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) for severe ARDS, and invasive cardiac output monitoring.
*   **The African Reality**: Advanced critical-care modalities (ECMO, CVVH) are strictly limited to a tiny number of tertiary academic centers [250].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: Focus on low-technology lung-protective ventilation (low tidal volume of **7 mL/kg**, plateau pressures $< 30$ cm H2O) [36, 153] and avoid high-PEEP recruitment strategies that can cause severe cardiovascular collapse in hypovolemic patients [249].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **ART Trial (Incremental PEEP Strategy)** which demonstrated that aggressive, high-PEEP lung recruitment maneuvers significantly increase mortality compared to low PEEP strategies [249].

### 13. European Society of Cardiology (ESC) — Cardiovascular Diseases & Hypertensive Emergencies
*   **The Global Standard**: Promotes immediate coronary angiography/PCI for acute coronary syndromes (ACS), advanced echocardiography for heart failure, and immediate MAP lowering in hypertensive crises.
*   **The African Reality**: PCI-capable catheterization laboratories are virtually non-existent in the public sector outside major metropolitan hubs; acute cardiac tamponade is highly prevalent and frequently infectious (Tuberculosis) [34, 139].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: For acute STEMI, early **pharmacological thrombolysis (e.g., streptokinase or tenecteplase)** remains the gold-standard rescue therapy in the ED [2]. For acute tamponade secondary to TB pericarditis, utilize emergency **ultrasound-guided pericardiocentesis with a 16-gauge IV cannula** or indwelling pigtail catheter as a safe bedside rescue technique [32, 140, 276].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **IMPI (Investigation of the Management of Pericarditis in Africa) Trial (Mayosi et al.)**: Defined the diagnosis and treatment of tuberculous pericarditis, highlighting the lack of benefit of routine steroid adjunctive therapy in reducing mortality, though they decrease constrictive pericarditis [35, 141, 142].

## Section 2: Cardiovascular, Stroke, & Thrombosis (Guidelines 14-17)

### 14. American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology (AHA/ACC) — Acute Cardiovascular Care
*   **The Global Standard**: Immediate percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) within 90 minutes for STEMI, high-sensitivity troponin algorithms for NSTEMI, and advanced pharmacotherapy for cardiogenic shock.
*   **The African Reality**: Lack of catheterization labs and high prevalence of non-atherosclerotic cardiac disease (valvular, peripartum cardiomyopathy, and TB pericarditis) [33, 141].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: Rule out infectious mimicking conditions (such as tuberculous pericarditis presenting with electrical alternans on ECG) before administering heparin or antiplatelet agents, which can precipitate catastrophic hemopericardium and tamponade [13, 33, 141, 275].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **Naicker et al. TBP Update (2018)**: Detailing that specialized cardiac MRI and FDG-PET imaging, though recommended by ESC/AHA guidelines for pericardial disease, are functionally useless in high-prevalence African district settings due to extreme wait times [276].

### 15. American Heart Association/American Stroke Association (AHA/ASA) — Acute Ischaemic Stroke & Hemorrhage
*   **The Global Standard**: Mandates rapid head CT scan, thrombolysis with alteplase/tenecteplase within 4.5 hours of ischemic stroke onset, and strict BP targets (< 185/110 mmHg) [172, 175].
*   **The African Reality**: Patients present extremely late (well beyond the 4.5-hour thrombolysis window) [191, 194], and CT scanners are frequently broken or geographically inaccessible [273].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**:
    *   *Stroke BP Rules*: Strictly **withhold all antihypertensive therapy** in acute ischemic stroke unless the blood pressure exceeds **220/120 mmHg** [175], at which point a cautious 15% reduction over 24 hours is targeted to preserve cerebral perfusion to the penumbra [175].
    *   *Eclampsia BP Rules*: For eclampsia or severe pre-eclampsia, immediately reduce SBP to **140–150 mmHg** and DBP to **90–100 mmHg** using rapid-acting oral nifedipine (10 mg tablet, swallowed whole) to prevent maternal hemorrhagic stroke [80].
    *   *Aortic Dissection BP Rules*: For acute aortic dissection, rapidly reduce SBP to **< 120 mmHg** and HR to **< 60 bpm** within 20 minutes [176], administering IV beta-blockers (labetalol) *before* vasodilators to prevent reflex tachycardia [176].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **INTERACT2 or ATACH-2 Trials**: Showing that smooth, titrated SBP control (minimizing blood pressure variability [BPV]) in acute intracranial hemorrhage prevents hematoma expansion without inducing cerebral ischemia [166, 170, 177].

### 16. CHEST Guidelines — Venous Thromboembolism (VTE) & Pulmonary Embolism
*   **The Global Standard**: Recommends early low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) or direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) for VTE, and advanced thrombolysis for submassive/massive PE.
*   **The African Reality**: High prevalence of co-existing tuberculosis and HIV, which alters baseline hypercoagulability and drug-drug interactions (e.g., rifampicin is a potent CYP3A4 inducer that drastically lowers DOAC levels) [41, 119].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: Avoid DOACs in patients receiving TB treatment (rifampicin-containing regimens) [119]. Prioritize standard, monitored **unfractionated heparin (UFH)** or weight-adjusted subcutaneous **Enoxaparin (LMWH)** [42].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **South African Hypertension and VTE Audits** highlighting that anticoagulation selection must be strictly integrated with the patient's infectious co-treatment profiles [42, 120].

### 17. American Society of Hematology (ASH) — Venous Thromboembolism & Anticoagulation
*   **The Global Standard**: Recommends complex lab-guided heparin titrations, direct thrombin inhibitors, and routine genetic testing for thrombophilia.
*   **The African Reality**: Turnaround times for basic coagulation panels (PT/aPTT) exceed several hours, and specialized assays are completely unavailable [50, 54, 190].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: Implement **Enoxaparin weight-standardized prophylaxis** for post-admission patients [42]. For post-natal patients with eclampsia/pre-eclampsia, enoxaparin dosing is strictly weight-bracketed: **20 mg daily** (<50 kg), **40 mg daily** (50–90 kg), **60 mg daily** (91–130 kg), and **80 mg daily** (131–170 kg) [42].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **Grey's Hospital Blood Product Audit (Visser et al.)** showing that waiting for formal laboratory coagulation results in actively bleeding patients leads to delayed, reactive, and sub-optimal hematological management [50, 51].

## Section 3: Trauma, Emergency Surgery, & Neurotrauma (Guidelines 18-26)

### 18. American College of Surgeons Trauma Quality Programs (ACS-TQP) — Trauma Resuscitation & TBI
*   **The Global Standard**: Promotes high-technology trauma resuscitation, immediate head CT for traumatic brain injury (TBI), and aggressive intracranial pressure (ICP) monitoring.
*   **The African Reality**: High burden of penetrating neurotrauma and absolute lack of invasive ICP monitors in provincial hospitals [79, 114].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: Brain CT scans are **unreliable for detecting raised ICP** and should not be performed simply to exclude it [79, 115, 117]. Instead, closely monitor for clinical signs of impending brain herniation (sudden drop in GCS, relative bradycardia, hypertension, or pupillary asymmetry) and immediately initiate hyperosmolar rescue therapy: **3% hypertonic saline (3 mL/kg over 15 minutes)** or **20% mannitol (0.5 to 1 g/kg over 15 minutes)** [116, 129, 211].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **CRASH-3 Trial (2019)**: Proved that early administration of **tranexamic acid (TXA) is highly effective and safe** in mild-to-moderate traumatic brain injury if administered within 3 hours of injury.

### 19. Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma (EAST) — Trauma Practice-Management Guidelines
*   **The Global Standard**: Recommends extensive diagnostic imaging and multi-specialty operative interventions for blunt and penetrating torso trauma.
*   **The African Reality**: Extreme surgical backlog and lack of available operating theaters, particularly overnight [191].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: For hemodynamically stable penetrating abdominal trauma without peritonitis, utilize structured serial clinical examinations over 24 hours (non-operative management) rather than immediate laparotomy to save scarce surgical resources [74, 126].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **Chris Hani Baragwanath Academic Hospital Trauma Audits** demonstrating that selective non-operative management of penetrating abdominal trauma is highly safe and significantly reduces negative laparotomy rates [74, 126].

### 20. World Society of Emergency Surgery (WSES) — General Surgery & Source Control
*   **The Global Standard**: Recommends immediate operative source control for all severe intra-abdominal infections, accompanied by expensive, broad-spectrum carbapenems.
*   **The African Reality**: Highly prevalent antimicrobial resistance and a complete lack of intensive care support post-operatively [10, 28].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: Source control must be prioritized using the least invasive option available (e.g., ultrasound-guided percutaneous drainage of intra-abdominal abscesses over open laparotomy) [247], combined with strict adherence to local antibiotic stewardship guidelines [42].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **Surgical Sepsis Outcomes in SA (2019)**: Demonstrated that prompt, low-technology surgical drainage in stable patients is far more critical for survival than high-cost antibiotic selection [45, 88].

### 21. American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (AAST) — Pelvic Ring & Torso Trauma
*   **The Global Standard**: Immediate pelvic angiography or emergency laparotomy with pelvic packing for hemodynamically unstable pelvic fractures.
*   **The African Reality**: Angiographic embolization is unavailable in 99% of African hospitals, and emergency pelvic external fixators are restricted to orthopaedic theaters [58, 60].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: Resuscitation is anchored strictly on **pelvic binders and haemostatic resuscitation** [58, 59]. Pelvic binders must be placed precisely over the **greater trochanters** [21]. Emergency personnel must **NEVER test for pelvic stability via manual springing** [58, 76], and patients must **NEVER be log-rolled until the pelvic ring is cleared** [58].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **UK Major Trauma Centre Pelvic Binder Assessment (Naseem et al.)** showing that up to 50% of pelvic binders are placed too high over the iliac crests, which is biomechanically harmful and fails to reduce pelvic volume [21].

### 22. Brain Trauma Foundation (BTF) — Severe Traumatic Brain Injury
*   **The Global Standard**: Recommends targeting a cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) of 60–70 mmHg guided by invasive parenchymal ICP monitors.
*   **The African Reality**: Complete absence of ICP monitoring equipment [79, 114].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: Maintain SBP $\ge 110$ mmHg (or age-appropriate targets in children) using titrated isotonic crystalloids and vasopressors [2]. Elevate the head of the bed to 30 degrees [129, 211], optimize oxygenation, and monitor pupillary responses and GCS hourly [129, 214] as primary clinical surrogates for CPP [79, 129].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **BEST-TRIP Trial (Chestnut et al., 2012)**: Multi-center RCT in South America showing that ICP-monitor-guided care does not improve outcomes compared to care guided by serial clinical exams and CT imaging.

### 23. Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA 2026) — Acute Asthma Assessment & Management
*   **The Global Standard**: Focuses on rapid, repetitive administration of inhaled short-acting beta-agonists (SABA) and inhaled corticosteroids (ICS), backed by quantitative peak expiratory flow (PEF) measurements.
*   **The African Reality**: Peak flow meters are rarely functional in public EDs, and nebulizer chambers are frequently out of stock [151].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: Rely on clinical severity signs (ability to speak in full sentences, accessory muscle use, silent chest) [152, 178]. If nebulizers are unavailable, administer SABA via a **metered-dose inhaler (MDI) attached to a simple, improvised spacer** (e.g., a clean 500 mL plastic water bottle), which has demonstrated clinical equivalence to wet nebulization [59].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **Systematic Reviews of MDI Spacer vs. Nebulisation** proving that spacers are highly cost-effective and clinically non-inferior in acute pediatric and adult asthma exacerbations.

### 24. Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD 2026) — COPD Exacerbations
*   **The Global Standard**: Recommends early initiation of non-invasive positive pressure ventilation (NIPPV) for respiratory acidosis, and immediate dual bronchodilator therapy.
*   **The African Reality**: High baseline rates of chronic destructive pulmonary tuberculosis (TB) that mimic or co-exist with COPD, and high risk of secondary pneumothorax if positive pressure ventilation is used unselectively [41, 119].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: Maintain a **cautious oxygen saturation target of 88% to 92%** [216]. Always obtain a chest radiograph *before* initiating positive pressure ventilation (such as CPAP or BiPAP) to exclude an active **Secondary Spontaneous Pneumothorax (SSP)**, as positive airway pressure can rapidly convert an SSP into a fatal tension pneumothorax [215, 216].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **Duchman et al. Pleural Review (2023)**: Detailing that standard oxygen delivery methods introducing positive airway pressures must be strictly avoided in patients with active or suspected alveolopleural fistulas [39, 179].

### 25. European Respiratory Society/American Thoracic Society (ERS/ATS) — Acute Respiratory Failure
*   **The Global Standard**: Focuses on early NIPPV, high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) therapy, and rapid progression to mechanical ventilation in ICU.
*   **The African Reality**: Ward-based HFNC and NIPPV are limited by oxygen flow constraints and lack of nursing staff to monitor for clinical deterioration [10, 191].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: Strictly reserve NIPPV/HFNC for cooperative patients without copious secretions [1]. If the patient exhibits signs of exhaustion or worsening acidosis on serial clinical gases, early elective intubation by an experienced physician using a **bougie-assisted rapid sequence intubation (RSI)** is preferred over delayed, emergency intubation in extremis [152, 153].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **South African Pediatric and Adult Airway Audits** emphasizing that a standardized, bougie-first RSI protocol minimizes intubation attempts and prevents hypoxic cardiac arrest [152, 153].

### 26. British Thoracic Society (BTS 2023) — Spontaneous Pneumothorax & Pleural Disease
*   **The Global Standard**: The updated 2023 guidelines represent a major paradigm shift, **de-emphasizing absolute radiographic size thresholds** in favor of a symptom-and-physiology-driven approach [78].
*   **The African Reality**: In regions with a massive tuberculosis (TB) and HIV burden, pneumothoraces are frequently **Secondary Spontaneous Pneumothoraces (SSP)** secondary to cavitary lung disease [41, 119]. Unlike PSPs, SSPs carry a high risk of continuous air leak and rapid physiological deterioration due to extremely low pulmonary reserve [215].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**:
    *   *Primary Spontaneous Pneumothorax (PSP)*: For stable patients with minimal or no symptoms, **conservative observation and simple analgesia are first-line**, regardless of radiographic size [96].
    *   *Symptomatic/Compromised PSP*: **Simple manual needle aspiration via a three-way tap** is the preferred first-line intervention [101], achieving successful lung re-expansion in 50% to 80% of cases [216].
    *   *Persistent Air Leak (PAL)*: Defined by continuous bubbling in the water seal bottle of an ICD beyond **5 to 7 days** [87]. If thoracic surgery is unavailable, perform **medical autologous blood pleurodesis ("blood patch")** by instilling **1 to 2 mL/kg of the patient's own venous blood** directly into the pleural cavity through the chest tube to seal the fistula [87].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **Brown et al. PSP Conservative Management Trial (NEJM, 2020)**: Landmark RCT proving that conservative observation of large, stable PSPs is non-inferior to invasive intervention, with fewer complications and shorter hospital stays.

## Section 4: Infectious Diseases & Meningitis (Guidelines 27-29)

### 27. Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) — Meningitis & Sepsis Antibiotics
*   **The Global Standard**: Focuses on immediate, high-dose empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics (e.g., ceftriaxone, vancomycin) within 1 hour of suspected bacterial meningitis.
*   **The African Reality**: High prevalence of tuberculous (TBM) and cryptococcal meningitis (CM) which present sub-acutely, where standard bacterial regimens are completely ineffective [41, 119].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: For patients presenting with a less acute history (headache, fever, weight loss, or cranial nerve palsies over 5 to 30 days), immediately suspect **TBM or CM** [118, 119]. Perform a lumbar puncture (LP) to measure opening pressure, obtain CSF CrAg, and run GeneXpert MTB/RIF [104, 106, 273].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **GeneXpert MTB/RIF Extrapulmonary Validation Studies (Cochrane, 2018)**: Validating the high specificity of molecular rapid testing for extrapulmonary TB in CSF specimens [277].

### 28. European Society of Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases (ESCMID) — Antimicrobial Stewardship
*   **The Global Standard**: Mandates strict antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) guidelines, restricting carbapenems and third-generation cephalosporins in the ED.
*   **The African Reality**: High rates of multi-drug resistant (MDR) organisms in public hospitals due to severe overcrowding and lack of isolation facilities [10, 17].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: Implement a **tiered empirical antibiotic protocol** based on local resistance patterns and the patient's immune status [70]. In stable patients with low likelihood of infection and without shock, **defer antimicrobial therapy** while continuing to closely monitor [235].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **Grey's Hospital Surgical Sepsis Stewardship Audits** showing that implementing localized, restricted prescribing guides successfully limits the spread of Extended-Spectrum Beta-Lactamase (ESBL) producing pathogens [45, 88].

### 29. WHO Infectious-Disease Guidelines — Tuberculosis, HIV, Rabies, & Malaria
*   **The Global Standard**: Recommends standard 9-month treatments for extra-pulmonary tuberculosis and immediate post-exposure prophylaxis for rabies.
*   **The African Reality**: South Africa runs a national reflex laboratory CrAg screening program for all CD4 < 200 cells/µL to catch asymptomatic cryptococcal infection [116, 227].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**:
    *   *Cryptococcal Meningitis (SAHCS 2026 update)*: First-line induction is a **single high-dose of liposomal amphotericin B (10 mg/kg) on Day 1**, combined with **14 days of oral flucytosine (100 mg/kg/day) and oral fluconazole (1200 mg/day)** [116]. **ART must be delayed for 4 to 6 weeks** after CM diagnosis [106] to prevent fatal IRIS (Ugandan trials showed a 15% absolute mortality increase with early ART).
    *   *Rabies Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (NICD Guidelines)*: category III exposures (skin breach or bat contact) require immediate wound washing with soap and running water for **5 to 10 minutes** [151]. **Avoid or delay wound suturing and strictly avoid local anesthetics**, as these spread the virus locally [151]. Infiltrate **Rabies Immunoglobulin (RIG)** directly in and around the wound [151]. RIG is **strictly contraindicated after 7 days** from the first vaccine dose, as active antibodies are already developing [151].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **AMBITION-cm Trial (NEJM, 2022)**: Proved that single high-dose liposomal amphotericin B is non-inferior to 7-day deoxycholate regimens for cryptococcal meningitis, with significantly fewer toxicities [116].

## Section 5: Renal, Diabetes, & Malnutrition (Guidelines 30-36)

### 30. Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO 2026) — Acute Kidney Injury (AKI)
*   **The Global Standard**: Recommends continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) for severe stage 3 AKI, guided by continuous invasive hemodynamic monitoring.
*   **The African Reality**: Bedside hemodialysis is severely restricted, and patients with chronic renal disease or poor socio-economic support are frequently excluded from chronic dialysis programs [42].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: Focus on identifying reversible toxic causes of AKI, particularly **Traditional African Medicine (TAM) / Muthi poisoning** [42, 143]. Use bedside **renal ultrasound** to differentiate acute kidney injury (kidneys normal in size but bright/echogenic) from chronic kidney disease (shrunken kidneys with cortical thinning) [143, 278].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **KovsieScholar Pediatric AKI Audits** showing that early recognition of toxic nephropathy and rapid correction of severe hyperkalemia prevents the need for emergency dialysis [115, 278].

### 31. American Diabetes Association/EASD — Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA) & HHS Consensus
*   **The Global Standard**: Recommends aggressive fluid resuscitation (1 L of 0.9% NaCl in the first hour), immediate insulin boluses (0.1 units/kg), followed by continuous insulin infusions [126].
*   **The African Reality**: Unselected fluid and insulin boluses in pediatric DKA trigger rapid osmotic shifts, leading to fatal **clinical cerebral injury (edema)** in up to 1% of pediatric cases (which carries a 20% to 25% mortality rate) [126].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**:
    *   *Pediatric DKA Rules (< 14 years or < 50 kg)*:
        1.  **NEVER give an insulin bolus** [126].
        2.  **NEVER give IV sodium bicarbonate** (unless life-threatening hyperkalemia) [126].
        3.  **NEVER give fluid boluses if not in clinical shock** [126, 134].
        4.  Deficit must be replaced slowly over **36 to 48 hours** [126] using simple weight-based rates: **5 mL/kg/h** ($\le 15$ kg), **4 mL/kg/h** (16–40 kg), and **3 mL/kg/h** ($> 40$ kg) [126].
        5.  Initiate regular insulin infusion at **0.05 to 0.1 units/kg/hour** starting **1 to 2 hours AFTER fluid resuscitation** has commenced [126].
    *   *Adult DKA Rules ($> 14$ years or $> 50$ kg)*:
        1.  Calculate serum osmolality ($2 	imes [	ext{Na}^+] + 	ext{Glucose} + 	ext{Urea}$) and aim for a gradual decline of **5 mOsmol/kg/hour** to prevent cerebral edema [126, 211].
        2.  Corrected sodium must not fall faster than **10 mmol/L over 24 hours**.
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **MOH Saudi Pediatric DKA Protocol Consensus** which validated these strict, conservative fluid and insulin limits to eliminate the incidence of treatment-induced cerebral edema [126, 208].

### 32. Endocrine Society — Adrenal Crisis & Severe Hyperglycaemia
*   **The Global Standard**: Focuses on immediate, high-dose intravenous hydrocortisone (100 mg), aggressive fluid resuscitation, and continuous laboratory endocrinology panels.
*   **The African Reality**: Intravenous hydrocortisone is frequently out of stock, and chronic adrenal insufficiency is heavily driven by undiagnosed, chronic tuberculosis of the adrenal glands (Addison's disease) [41, 119].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: Maintain a high index of suspicion for adrenal crisis in stable patients presenting with refractory shock, hyperkalemia, hyponatremia, and hyperpigmentation [2]. If hydrocortisone is unavailable, administer **intravenous dexamethasone (4 mg to 8 mg)** as a safe, highly potent glucocorticoid rescue alternative that does not interfere with serum cortisol assays [2].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **South African National TB Guidelines** detailing the systemic, endocrine, and extra-pulmonary manifestations of tuberculosis in endemic populations [119, 223].

### 33. American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) — Febrile Infants & Bronchiolitis
*   **The Global Standard**: Recommends exhaustive lumbar punctures and septic workups for all febrile infants under 21–28 days, and strictly advises against bronchodilators in bronchiolitis.
*   **The African Reality**: High prevalence of vertical HIV exposure, making infant immune status highly complex; extreme overcrowding in pediatric wards makes viral cross-transmission (bronchiolitis) a major hazard [41, 119].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: For febrile infants with known or suspected HIV exposure, **broaden empirical antibiotic coverage** to include ceftriaxone and ampicillin early [42]. In bronchiolitis, if respiratory distress is severe and HFNC is unavailable, perform a **trial of nebulized hypertonic saline (3%) or a single trial of nebulized adrenaline**, which can improve clinical clearance in a subset of patients [42].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **Western Cape Pediatric Clinical Protocols** outlining risk-adapted septic workup strategies for infants based on local HIV and TB exposure risk profiles [62, 114].

### 34. Canadian Paediatric Society (CPS) — Fever, Safeguarding, & Acute Care
*   **The Global Standard**: Focuses on child safeguarding indicators, structured social work-ups, and advanced toxicological screening.
*   **The African Reality**: Lack of toxicological laboratory services and extremely high baseline rates of severe acute malnutrition (SAM) [104, 105].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: Integrate safeguarding audits with **nutritional status screening** (using MUAC and checking for bilateral pitting nutritional edema) [104, 105]. In malnourished children, do not utilize standard pediatric dehydration charts, as loss of subcutaneous fat mimics dehydration [104].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **KovsieScholar SAM Cohort Study** demonstrating that social deprivation and poverty are the strongest independent predictors of admission complications and refeeding syndrome in public pediatric wards [115].

### 35. WHO Emergency Triage, Assessment, and Treatment (ETAT) & Basic Emergency Care
*   **The Global Standard**: Structured triage system designed specifically for resource-constrained clinics and district hospitals to identify children in need of immediate emergency, priority, or non-urgent care.
*   **The African Reality**: The core operational standard in African public health, as it relies entirely on clinical signs (e.g., chest in-drawing, stridor, cold extremities) without requiring any laboratory or radiologic inputs [10, 191].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: Strictly implement **ETAT priority signs** (e.g., severe wasting, bilateral pitting edema, severe pallor) to fast-track children straight to resuscitation beds [104, 105].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **Maitland et al. ETAT Implementation Audits** showing that systematic ETAT triage training for nursing staff reduces inpatient pediatric mortality by up to 30%.

### 36. AHA/ERC Paediatric Life-Support Guidelines — Paediatric Resuscitation & Neonatal Care
*   **The Global Standard**: Focuses on rapid, high-resource pediatric advanced life support (PALS), utilizing continuous epinephrine infusions and immediate post-resuscitation therapeutic hypothermia.
*   **The African Reality**: Refeeding Syndrome (RFS) characterized by hypophosphatemia is highly prevalent, occurring in **8.7% to 15% of children hospitalized with SAM** in South Africa [82, 115], driven by reductive metabolic adaptation [83].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**:
    *   *Pediatric Refeeding Syndrome (RFS)*: Defined as **new or worsening hypophosphatemia occurring within 72 hours of starting feeds** (serum phosphate below age-specific limits or $< 0.65$–0.80 mmol/L, or a $\ge 20\%$ fall from baseline) [83, 115].
    *   *Prevention Protocol*: Feed strictly with **F-75 formula** (stabilization feed) [83]. **NEVER use diuretics to treat nutritional edema** [83]. Strictly **withhold iron** during the stabilization phase (prevents bacterial overgrowth and oxidative tissue injury) [83]. Supplement potassium (oral Mist Pot Chloride **1 mL/kg 8-hourly**) early [83].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **GASTROSAM Trial (Maitland et al., 2021)**: Phase II RCT demonstrating that **cautious intravenous rehydration (100 mL/kg Ringer's Lactate over 3 to 8 hours)** in dehydrated SAM children is safe, does not precipitate congestive heart failure, and significantly improves survival over standard oral rehydration [68, 122].

## Section 6: Obstetric & Gynaecological Resuscitation (Guidelines 37-40)

### 37. Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (RCOG) Green-top Guidelines
*   **The Global Standard**: Extensive post-partum hemorrhage (PPH) management utilizing immediate balloon tamponade, uterine artery embolization, and immediate ICU admission.
*   **The African Reality**: Obstetric hemorrhage is the number one cause of maternal mortality worldwide, with massive shortages of ICU beds and specialized surgical interventions in rural areas [280].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: Resuscitation is structured around the South African **ESMOE (Essential Steps in Managing Obstetric Emergencies)** protocol [80]. PPH is managed immediately using a low-technology **Uterine Balloon Tamponade (UBT)** (improvised using a standard Foley catheter and a condom filled with sterile saline) [42].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **WOMAN Trial (Lancet, 2017)**: Proved that **1 g of Tranexamic Acid (TXA) given intravenously within 3 hours** of delivery reduces death due to bleeding in post-partum hemorrhage by over 30% without increasing thromboembolic risk [286].

### 38. WHO Maternal and Perinatal Guidance — Hypertensive Disorders & PPH
*   **The Global Standard**: Recommends extensive lab-guided surveillance of severe pre-eclampsia and immediate caesarean delivery for fetal distress.
*   **The African Reality**: Maternal pulmonary edema occurs in over 30% of maternal eclampsia deaths in public facilities, almost always precipitated by unselected, aggressive intravenous fluid administration [80].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: Resuscitate using the ESMOE systematic approach (stabilize Mother *before* any fetal assessment) [80]. Target SBP **140–150 mmHg** and DBP **90–100 mmHg** using rapid oral nifedipine (10 mg) [80]. Crucially, **strictly restrict fluid therapy to a total of 80 mL/hour** (both oral and IV fluids combined) to prevent fatal pulmonary edema [80].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **ESMOE National Audits (South Africa)** showing that implementing strict fluid restrictions and standardized eclampsia drills significantly slashes maternal mortality [42, 80, 121].

### 39. International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) — Obstetric Consensus
*   **The Global Standard**: Recommends routine carbetocin for PPH prevention, molecular thrombophilia screening, and immediate access to operative suites.
*   **The African Reality**: Carbetocin is highly expensive and unavailable; oxytocin and misoprostol are subject to cold-chain failures in rural clinics.
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: Prevention of PPH in the ED utilizes stable, cheap alternatives: **oxytocin (10 IU IM or slow IV infusion)** and **oral/rectal misoprostol (600 mcg to 800 mcg)** [2, 42].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **WHO CHAMPION Trial**: Validated heat-stable carbetocin as equivalent to oxytocin, highlighting the critical role of cold-chain-independent uterotonics in low-resource settings.

### 40. American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) — Pregnancy Emergencies
*   **The Global Standard**: Detailed guidance on managing ectopic pregnancy, early pregnancy loss, and gestational hypertension using advanced laparoscopy and neonatal support.
*   **The African Reality**: Lack of surgical theater access for ectopic pregnancies and lack of advanced neonatal intensive care [28, 42].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: Ectopic pregnancies presenting with clinical shock must undergo immediate **emergency bedside ultrasound (POCUS)** to confirm free intraperitoneal fluid [142]. Ressuscitate aggressively with blood and blood products [51], and perform an emergency auto-transfusion of blood collected from the peritoneal cavity if commercial blood products are out of stock [42, 51].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **South African Maternal Death Reports (Saving Mothers)** indicating that delays in recognizing ruptured ectopic pregnancies and slow initiation of blood transfusions are major preventable causes of death.

## Section 7: Clinical Toxicology & Poisoning (Guidelines 41-43)

### 41. American Academy of Clinical Toxicology (AACT) — Gastrointestinal Decontamination
*   **The Global Standard**: Restricts activated charcoal use to within 1 hour of ingestion, and strictly advises against multi-dose activated charcoal (MDAC) or gastric lavage.
*   **The African Reality**: Patients present extremely late, and pesticide ingestions (such as organophosphates and carbamates) are highly lethal and delay gastric emptying [1, 2].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**: Administer **activated charcoal (50 g for adults, 1 g/kg for children)** mixed as a slurry with water, strictly *only* when the airway is protected [1]. Charcoal can be given beyond the 1-hour window if the ingested toxin is known to delay gastric emptying (e.g., organophosphates) [1].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **NEMLC South African Primary Care EML Guidelines (2020 Update)**: Formally updated activated charcoal safety parameters and dosing tables for public primary-care emergency units [1, 2].

### 42. European Association of Poisons Centres and Clinical Toxicologists (EAPCCT) — Antidotes
*   **The Global Standard**: Recommends immediate administration of high-cost antidotes (e.g., pralidoxime/obidoxime for organophosphates, DigiFab for cardiac glycoside toxicity).
*   **The African Reality**: High-cost antidotes (pralidoxime, DigiFab) are **virtually unavailable in South African public hospitals** due to prohibitive costs and supply chain gaps [2, 38].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**:
    *   *Organophosphate Poisoning*: Prioritize **atropine titration and supportive ventilation** [2]. Administer oxygen to target **SpO2 > 94% BEFORE giving atropine** to prevent fatal ventricular arrhythmias [152]. Avoid succinylcholine (suxamethonium) during rapid sequence intubation [152, 153].
    *   *Traditional Medicine (TAM) Cardiotoxicity*: In yellow tulp (*Moraea pallida*) poisoning, the toxic compound **epoxyscillirosidine** acts as a cardiotoxic cardiac glycoside [122]. Digoxin immunoassays cross-react with circulating bufadienolides, providing qualitative diagnosis [38]. Resuscitate with magnesium, correct hyperkalemia [278], and support hemodynamics [38].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **Botha et al. Moraea Pallida Toxicity Studies**: Established the spatial variation and extreme potency of bufadienolide toxins in South Africa, reinforcing the need for rapid clinical identification in EDs [122].

### 43. American College of Medical Toxicology (ACMT) — Antidotes & Opioid Toxicity
*   **The Global Standard**: Mandates immediate intravenous N-acetylcysteine (NAC) infusions for paracetamol overdose, and immediate intravenous naloxone infusions for opioid overdose.
*   **The African Reality**: Paracetamol is the most common agent in deliberate self-poisoning in SA [137]. Districts frequently lack intravenous NAC formulations, and patients presenting with "nyaope" overdoses exhibit complex neuropsychiatric toxicities due to efavirenz adulteration [41, 65, 96].
*   **Local Adaptation ("What to say on rounds")**:
    *   *Paracetamol Overdose*: If IV NAC is unavailable, **oral N-acetylcysteine (140 mg/kg loading, followed by 70 mg/kg 4-hourly for 17 doses)** represents a safe, validated, and life-saving alternative [2, 3]. **Avoid giving activated charcoal if oral NAC is administered**, as charcoal will bind oral NAC and negate its systemic absorption [3].
    *   *Nyaope Overdose*: Resuscitate with titrated naloxone for opioid-induced respiratory depression, but prepare for severe agitation and psychosis during reversal due to efavirenz and stimulant adulterants [95, 96].
*   **Key Trial to Name-Drop**: **South African Paracetamol Poisoning Retrospective Review (SAMA Journals)**: Demonstrated that oral NAC is highly effective at preventing hepatic necrosis when tertiary referral is delayed [137].
